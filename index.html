Innovation
How effectively are grantees innovating, including with AI, to increase cost-effectiveness?
Innovation · Org-wide
The techlash is not a wholesale rejection of edtech, but districts are becoming less forgiving of products that cannot show evidence of impact and convert it into effective implementation.

New York City restricted broad student-facing generative AI while allowing limited, vetted high-school pilots and assessing tools for instructional impact, prior research, safety, and privacy. More broadly, a Learning Agency analysis of 51 state technology bills found that none of the eight most restrictive proposals passed; enacted policies generally favored guardrails or exceptions for instructional interventions over outright bans. Together, these signals suggest that policymakers are distinguishing purposeful educational uses of technology from undirected screen use, with evidence increasingly important to that distinction even as research struggles to keep pace with the market.

OFF's evidence shows the same selectivity among buyers. OnYourMark lost renewals in its weakest implementations; PLI findings contributed to continuation in New Mexico and reassessment in Chicago; and Quill used promising Fishtank results to support expansion, although free reach and curriculum integration did not translate into expected paid adoption. ASSISTments retained only districts with direct relationships, while strong study results could not prevent one district from leaving when its technology strategy changed. Durable adoption therefore appears to require credible evidence plus alignment with instructional priorities, district systems, implementation capacity, and buyer decision-making criteria.

Implication
Diligence should assess whether grantees can articulate a testable theory of how their tool changes educator or student behavior — not just whether it is used.
Innovation · Org-wide
AI can make classroom evidence more timely and actionable for professional learning, but its ability to translate that evidence into improved teaching without human coaching remains uncertain.

AI shows particular promise in shortening the path from classroom activity to usable evidence. TeachFX and M-Powering Teachers converted recordings into concrete signals, including talk ratios, wait time, questioning, uptake of student ideas, and student reasoning. TeachFX's "counts before context" routine helped coaches and teachers use quantitative signals to deepen discussion of instruction, while M-Powering coaches could locate relevant moments without reviewing entire lessons.

Early findings suggest these tools can strengthen coaching cycles, but they do not establish consistent effects on teaching or cost. In a small M-Powering study, participants reported improvement in their selected practices, 71% said coaching influenced their in-the-moment instruction, and more than half of coaches reported saving time. However, these findings were descriptive and self-reported, and savings remained limited because interpreting evidence and conducting coaching conversations still required substantial human effort. TeachFX likewise helped coaches initiate richer analysis, but scheduling and implementation barriers left four of nine schools without enough usable data.

Evidence for AI in a more direct coaching role is mixed. NISA's randomized prompt did not credibly improve average student-centeredness, user perceptions, or time savings, although prompted coaches improved across successive conversations on a platform-based measure. General-purpose ChatGPT sometimes misread strong mathematics instruction and recommended moves that could reduce cognitive demand. By contrast, LENA reports early positive results from replacing some live coaching with AI-generated insights, but its comparative effectiveness and cost advantage require broader validation.

The evidence therefore supports AI more strongly as a tool for making evidence gathering and feedback timely and specific than as an autonomous source of instructional judgment. Human-led models have stronger evidence, while the potential for substitution remains unresolved.

Implication
Favor AI investments that specify a complete coaching pathway: classroom evidence → human interpretation → curriculum-connected action → another opportunity to practice and receive feedback. "Generates feedback" is not an adequate theory of change.
Implementation
What are the primary barriers to quality implementation at scale and which supports most reliably ensure interventions reach required dosage for student-level impact?
Implementation · Org-wide
Cost-effective scale with quality depends on redesigning delivery, not simply reducing support: make routine work more efficient and focus human expertise on high-leverage activities.

Every Child Ready reduced cost per student by 26% through standardized professional learning while continuing to provide more intensive support where local instructional leadership was weak. CommonLit's implementation framework, readiness guides, and usage monitoring increased completion of its Getting Ready phase from 7% to 27.8%, suggesting that well-designed routine supports can reduce implementation friction. Teaching Lab is also testing AI-enabled tools, including NISA and Teaching Simulator, to lower the cost of coaching and professional learning while maintaining impact.

These examples provide different kinds of evidence. Every Child Ready documents a unit-cost reduction. CommonLit demonstrates an implementation improvement, but not a cost reduction. Teaching Lab represents a prospective efficiency strategy whose effects on cost and impact remain untested.

The evidence also cautions against equating lower staffing costs or greater reach with cost-effectiveness. BioBus supported 30% growth partly through greater reliance on junior staff, but Youth Voice and Reflection ratings weakened. This suggests that efficiencies can undermine quality when they displace the relational or instructional expertise responsible for impact.

The emerging principle is to automate or standardize repeatable work while preserving, and sometimes increasing, the human support needed for diagnosis, instructional judgment, relationship-building, and partners with limited local capacity.

Implication
Lower costs, reduced staff time, and greater reach are meaningful evidence of cost efficiency, even when their effects on impact are not yet known. They should not, however, be treated as evidence of cost-effectiveness. That judgment requires comparing the all-in cost of delivery with the dosage, quality, and outcomes achieved.
Implementation · Org-wide
District commitment produces meaningful dosage only when backed by implementation infrastructure: protected time, clear ownership, training and support, data monitoring, and accountability.

Across interventions, meaningful use was substantially higher when districts organized implementation around a defined instructional purpose. Zearn's assurance districts combined superintendent engagement, coaching, data transparency, protected routines, and launch expectations; weekly lesson completion grew 31%, compared with a 3% decline in non-assurance districts. Khan similarly converted 23–29% of licensed students to proficient use in districts organized around a specific instructional goal, versus 4–8% where participation remained optional.

A Khan field experiment, though in an international context, provides causal evidence for the importance of implementation infrastructure. Assigning one implementation owner to manage scheduling, rostering, teacher coordination, and progress monitoring increased weekly use from 7.2 minutes to 47.4 minutes and improved mathematics achievement.

Magpie shows how these conditions vary within U.S. districts. Ninety percent of Houston students met its usage threshold, compared with 40–50% in Baltimore, where scheduling disruptions, unclear district messaging, and concerns about instructional time weakened implementation. ASSISTments provides additional descriptive evidence: fidelity reached 19–20% through integration partners with stronger implementation infrastructure, compared with 8% in Maryland direct partnerships and 3% among lighter-support customers.

The same conditions appear to shape whether implementation persists after an external provider steps back. Jounce found more durable gains when school-level leader coaching was paired with network-level capacity, while PERTS found that site leadership explained more variation in improvement than incentives or course completion.

Implication
Grant agreements should specify both reach and dosage targets. External support should taper only after local leaders can independently protect time, onboard staff, monitor usage, resolve implementation challenges, and sustain accountability.
Implementation · Org-wide
Outcomes-based contracts can sharply increase usage, but they may organize implementation around dosage thresholds that have not been validated for impact.

Grantees define dosage in fundamentally different ways. CommonLit uses completion of at least half the lessons in three units; Zearn uses three grade-level lessons or approximately 90 minutes per week; Magpie has used 30- and 45-minute weekly thresholds; and Saga measures tutoring hours offered and received. These model-specific benchmarks help organizations set expectations and monitor participation, but they are not inherently comparable or necessarily evidence-based.

A Digital Promise and Center for Outcomes Based Contracting study demonstrates the operational power of such targets. Participating districts met provider-recommended dosage requirements for 50–95% of students and achieved usage rates approximately ten times those typically observed under traditional contracts. The strongest sites combined contractual expectations with mutual accountability, defined cross-functional roles, school-team involvement, real-time monitoring, and iterative implementation planning. Because these supports were bundled, the study does not isolate which elements increased usage. More importantly, it does not establish that the provider-recommended levels caused better student outcomes or represented the right dosage across students and contexts.

Evidence linking particular thresholds to outcomes remains limited. Among initially below-proficient CommonLit students, 33% of high-usage students ended proficient, compared with 27% of non-users. However, the comparison was descriptive, only 11% met the high-usage definition, and the evidence does not establish that the selected threshold caused the difference. The Personalized Learning Initiative reported a linear relationship between tutoring hours and academic gains, but its findings do not identify a universal threshold that applies across tutoring models or contexts. OnYourMark's stronger results in more aligned and collaborative schools further suggest that the same amount of exposure may not represent an equivalent instructional experience across settings.

Implication
Treat provider-defined dosage thresholds as provisional implementation hypotheses, not validated impact standards. Outcomes-based contracts can use these thresholds to clarify expectations and strengthen accountability, but they should separately track implementation quality and student outcomes. Where possible, variation in participation should be used to test which amounts and forms of engagement predict impact, for whom, and under what conditions.
Strategic partnerships
How can grantees form strategic partnerships to improve the delivery, impact, and sustainability of their models?
Strategic Partnerships · Org-wide
Distribution partnerships can generate new customers quickly, but their lasting value depends on adoption, renewal, and whether grantees can track what happens after the initial sale.

Several OFF grantees are using partners to reach customers they could not efficiently acquire through direct sales. Great Minds gives Magpie access to more than 150 sales representatives and opportunities potentially representing 30–40K students. ROAR's partnership with McGraw Hill similarly shifts sales and transactions to a commercial partner, allowing ROAR to focus on research, product development, and research-practice partnerships. These developments show that partnerships are opening doors, but leads, registrations, and introductions do not yet validate that customers will adopt and renew.

ASSISTments learned that partner-generated access is more likely to lead to renewal when paired with a direct relationship. Of six districts reached through Curriculum Sync, only two continued, and both already knew ASSISTments; the four newly introduced districts never engaged with the organization directly.

The Every Child Ready/Collaborative Classroom partnership offers a more comprehensive commercialization model to test. Collaborative Classroom is responsible for manufacturing, logistics, marketing, sales, onboarding, customer success, and data collection, while AppleTree retains responsibility for program design, research, and product improvement.

Implication
Track partner-sourced leads, conversion, onboarding, active use, renewal, revenue, margins, support costs, and the customer and usage data returned to the originating organization. Until those results are available, expanded pipelines should be treated as evidence of market access rather than durable scale.
Strategic Partnerships · Org-wide
Multi-provider partnerships are most likely to improve delivery when schools experience one coherent implementation model rather than several organizations' separate services.

Three examples illustrate a continuum from fragmentation to intentional integration. In the weakest model, ERS collaborated with Instruction Partners while New Solutions K12 separately provided scheduling guidance to another MSDE department. Without clear ownership across the providers and state offices, districts risked receiving inconsistent guidance. Leading Educators and Public Impact represent an adaptive model: when overlapping responsibilities among school and district roles reduced time for coaching, the partners clarified roles and aligned their training and supports. ASU Next Education Workforce and Instruction Partners demonstrate the most integrated model, jointly embedding team-based staffing and shared planning into HQIM unit and lesson internalization routines from the outset.

The emerging practice is to design partnerships from the school's point of view. This means aligning around one instructional outcome and shared theory of action, assigning a clear implementation lead, defining roles and decision rights, coordinating calendars and training, and using common measures.

Implication
Partners can retain distinct responsibilities for curriculum, staffing, coaching, data, or leadership development, but schools should encounter those contributions through one implementation plan rather than having to reconcile them independently.
Strategic Partnerships · Org-wide
OFF has the greatest influence before a partnership takes shape: early introductions can create new opportunities, while later involvement usually supports a deal others have already defined.

Every Child Ready and Collaborative Classroom connected at the Fall 2025 Early Impact convening and received follow-up coaching; they later formed an exclusive distribution partnership addressing AppleTree's limited sales capacity. An OFF breakfast prompted Teaching Lab/Public Impact and TN SCORE/Leading Educators to explore joint work. Blue Engine and Public Impact also connected at an OFF event and later secured $735,000 from the Oak Foundation. These examples suggest that OFF's convening power can catalyze partnerships and attract resources beyond its own funding.

When partnerships reached OFF later, its role was narrower. Zearn and Modern Classrooms requested funding for a largely formed pilot, while Magpie/Great Minds, ROAR/McGraw Hill, Quill/Fishtank, and Relay GSE/Teaching Lab informed OFF only after their partnerships were established. Grantees may hesitate to seek help, lack partnership-development capacity, or wait until external pressure makes partnership urgent.

Implication
Use convenings and regular strategy conversations to connect grantees with complementary needs and surface build, buy, or partner decisions early. Promising introductions can be followed with coaching to help grantees compare options and define roles without pressuring them to partner.
Evidence adoption
What tactics, investments, and partnerships most effectively support greater adoption of evidence-based practices and policies?
Evidence Adoption · Org-wide
Districts rarely connect the three stages needed for evidence-based procurement: defining success before purchase, tracking cost, use, and outcomes during delivery, and comparing options at renewal.

Edunomics' observations suggest a structural disconnect between district budgeting and evidence of effectiveness. Fewer than one-quarter of observed budget meetings mentioned student outcomes, and only 13% of renewal discussions considered whether an investment was working. Cost, outcomes, usage, and implementation therefore appear to enter decisions through separate conversations, if they enter at all. This helps explain why producing another study, dashboard, or evidence rating may have limited influence: the procurement process may not require anyone to compare an investment's results with its full cost, alternatives, or implementation requirements.

The strongest existing examples show evidence shaping decisions when it changes the terms of the decision itself. COBC incorporates accumulated contracting evidence into outcome measures, RFP templates, standards, and its Contract Wizard. 44% of contracts were developed without direct Center support, demonstrating that tools can diffuse beyond intensive technical assistance. But COBC has also found organizations using outcomes-based language without meeting its Standards of Excellence, showing that the appearance of evidence-based procurement can spread faster than the underlying discipline. Templates can change what purchasers ask for, but they require quality controls to ensure that evidence language affects contract design, measurement, and accountability.

PLI demonstrates what a functioning evidence-and-renewal loop can produce. Partner-specific findings contributed to New Mexico continuing tutoring, while Chicago paused to reassess and refine its approach after finding more muted effects. The important pattern is not that evidence consistently leads systems to continue or discontinue an investment. It is that the same evidence process can support different decisions based on local results, implementation, and priorities. This is closer to evidence adoption than applying a universal "works" label to a program.

OFF's newer investments are testing different parts of the infrastructure needed to make this routine. Edunomics is developing procurement and renewal tools that place cost per pupil, expected outcomes, usage, alternatives, and measurement responsibilities into the same decision process. NASLEE is testing whether research, peer learning, and targeted technical assistance lead state leaders to revise procurement criteria, curriculum-review processes, guidance, and implementation monitoring. CCSSO's seven-state Pre-K network addresses an earlier constraint: many states have expanded HQIM adoption without the data infrastructure needed to determine whether implementation is improving instruction or kindergarten readiness. Together, these investments test whether evidence can move from a separate research activity into the operating architecture for allocating resources.

Implication
Integrate cost, outcomes, usage, and implementation into the same decision process. Support procurement infrastructure that requires evidence comparison at renewal, not only at initial purchase.
AI @ OFF
How can OFF adopt and apply AI to promote efficiency and spending time on the highest value activities?
AI @ OFF · Org-wide
OFF team members are applying AI to save time and enhance their work, but these individual gains are not yet adding up to organizational value or transforming how we work together.

AI adoption broadened considerably in 2026. By year end, 100% of staff agreed AI had saved them time on key tasks, and the share who strongly agreed doubled from 25% at mid-year to 50%. 90% said it strengthened the quality of their work (up from 80%), and 75% said it expanded what they could do. Half estimate saving three or more hours a week. For Q2 sprints, most team members noted moderate to high gains for efficiency produced for the use cases they tested particularly for sensemaking-related tasks; ratings for effectiveness on the Q2 use case sprints were more varied but scored highest among those using AI for content generation.

Personal productivity is necessary and valuable but ultimately insufficient to generate organization-wide impacts. Confidence in AI's ability to meaningfully enhance OFF's work declined, with agreement falling from 75% at mid-year 2026 (and 65% at EOY 2025) to 53%. In team meetings, individuals noted the lack of projects and skills widely used across the team. Organization-wide value likely requires a few shared, high-frequency workflows, built once and used by many. This points to the importance of more intentional collaboration and change management aligned to developing and adopting shared workflows and skills along with tracking clearer indicators about how AI is influencing the quality and efficiency of these workflows. Meanwhile, one person's AI productivity may be another's AI slop, so we will benefit from developing clearer norms on how we engage with and utilize AI in consideration of downstream effects.

Implication
Develop shared workflows and norms before adding more individual AI tools. Track indicators about how AI influences quality and efficiency of workflows, not just time saved.
AI @ OFF · Org-wide
Knowledge management, not AI capability, is the binding constraint; AI can only synthesize what OFF has organized, and fragmented information is limiting the highest-value use cases.

The use cases staff value most depend on drawing context from many sources: synthesizing learning across portfolios, pressure-testing recommendations, and analyzing cost and impact. OFF's evidence is spread across Google Drive check-in notes, SharePoint, GivingData, Slack, Confluence, and emails. Participation in the Decoded Futures cohort and efforts to produce the Learning Synthesizer found that "retrospective aggregation is the single biggest time cost." It also found that without a source hierarchy, AI produces "broad themes rather than evidence-anchored insights." Other Q2 use cases hit the same wall:

  • The research repository had "no one source of truth."
  • The operations bot struggled because "information is fragmented across systems."
  • Grantee-report synthesis required a "heavy manual lift" of downloading documents from GivingData and re-uploading them.

This mirrors what OFF grantees learned through the AI Accelerator: "the tool is only as strong as the data and knowledge management system it's built on." More broadly, this elevates broader challenges with data and knowledge management and systems that transcend AI. EOY survey comments called for "one source of truth" amid a growing number of tracking spreadsheets, and only 61% of staff are confident in the quality of OFF Goals and grantee KPI data.

Implication
Treat knowledge management as AI infrastructure. Define sources of truth for key data, adopt shared naming and storage conventions, and connect core systems before adding more AI on top. Without that work, AI risks masking underlying process and data-design problems instead of fixing them. OFF is launching an engagement with Sattva in part to help address and document knowledge management roadblocks that it can seek to address in 2027.

HQIM
How does instructional coherence across Tier 1 materials, assessment, and professional learning drive student outcomes — and what features of HQIM implementation are most predictive of impact?
HQIM · School Success
Coherence is produced by a data-to-action system, not alignment alone

The stronger examples connect aligned content and assessments to an explicit instructional response: grouping, reteaching, intervention placement, or immediate student support. ROAR embeds screening results in NYCPS’s Ladder of Interventions; Zearn’s LiveView identifies students who are stalled or need a check-in during class; and ANet teachers used Compass data for grouping, pacing, and reteaching. The important counterexample is also ANet: teachers predominantly used the assessment retrospectively and made limited use of its predictive capabilities, constraining the intended connection between assessment and future curriculum decisions. TNTP’s practitioner evidence reinforces the importance of aligning Tier 2 content, goals, and instructional approaches with Tier 1, but does not causally isolate coherence from other system changes.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The portfolio evidence now points to a more specific mechanism: coherence depends on closing the loop from aligned information to a defined, feasible instructional action.

Implication for the portfolio

Diligence and grant reporting should examine the full loop: what data are generated, what decision they are meant to influence, whether that decision occurs, and whether students subsequently receive the intended support.

Sources · Stanford ROAR Grantee Notes · Zearn Grantee Notes · Achievement Network (ANet) Grantee Notes · Coherence by Design: Building Instructional Systems That Help Students Catch Up
HQIM · School Success
Assessments appear most useful when they reduce interpretation burden at the moment of instruction

The most promising features are short or embedded administration, curriculum-specific interpretation, real-time visibility, and reports that name a next step rather than simply present a score. Teachers described ROAR as fast and informative, but also identified opportunities to make its reports more actionable. Zearn’s LiveView responds to the burden of navigating multiple reports, while ASSISTments’ AI-generated summaries reportedly increased assigning by 134 percent among the least-frequent users. That engagement result does not establish that teachers made better decisions or that students learned more. ANet similarly shows that access to useful data does not ensure teachers use its most distinctive, forward-looking features.

What's new or sharper in 2026

“Low burden” is not merely shorter administration; it includes lowering the cognitive and workflow burden of deciding what to do next.

Implication for the portfolio

Assessment investments should measure report comprehension, time-to-decision, actions taken, and alignment of those actions to HQIM, in addition to completion and satisfaction.

Sources · Stanford ROAR Grantee Notes · Zearn Grantee Notes · ASSISTments Grantee Notes · Achievement Network (ANet) Grantee Notes
HQIM · School Success
Implementation ownership and embedded support matter more than access or generic training

Across ANet, Zearn, ASSISTments, and Khan Academy, stronger usage appears where schools have an explicit instructional goal, accountable leadership, recurring data routines, coaching, and a party responsible for follow-through. Zearn’s assurance districts recorded 31 percent growth in weekly lesson completion, compared with a 3 percent decline in non-assurance districts, although the bundled and observational design prevents attribution to a single component. ASSISTments reported fidelity of approximately 19–20 percent through integration partners, versus 8 percent in Maryland direct partnerships and 3 percent among lighter-support district customers. ANet found job-embedded coaching and established school data routines useful, while asynchronous modules alone had very low uptake.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The evidence increasingly distinguishes implementation infrastructure from support volume: more support is not automatically better, but ownership and support embedded in actual instructional routines appear consistently important.

Implication for the portfolio

Define minimum readiness and ownership conditions for each implementation pathway, then compare dosage, cost, and outcomes by pathway rather than treating all participating schools as equivalent.

HQIM · School Success
Dosage matters, but causal thresholds are still unproven

Multiple sources associate greater dosage with better outcomes, but operational definitions differ materially. Zearn uses three grade-level lessons or roughly 90 minutes weekly; CommonLit defines high usage as completing at least half the lessons in three units; and Saga’s NYC students received an average of 71 of 102 offered tutoring hours. Among initially below-proficient CommonLit students, 33 percent of high-usage students ended proficient versus 27 percent of non-users, but high usage included only 11 percent of students and the comparison was noncausal. PLI found a linear relationship between tutoring hours and learning gains, but did not establish a universal minimum threshold.

What's new or sharper in 2026

OFF now has stronger evidence that dosage should be monitored, but also stronger reasons not to transfer one organization’s cutoff to another model or population.

Implication for the portfolio

Require organizations to report the distribution of dosage, the percentage of target students reaching dosage, enabling conditions, outcomes by dosage band, and all-in cost per student reaching dosage.

Sources · Zearn Grantee Notes
HQIM · School Success
The portfolio can identify features associated with teacher practice, but not yet those most predictive of student achievement

Coaching, leadership routines, actionable data, and point-of-use support recur as predictors of implementation and teacher practice. The strongest causal caution comes from the AIR/Teaching Lab RCT: among participating treatment teachers, 20 hours of professional development and an average of 21.6 coaching sessions increased HMH self-efficacy by 0.48 SD and HMH use by 0.69 SD, but did not produce a statistically significant one-year achievement effect (0.09 SD, p=.116); 24 percent of treatment teachers refused participation amid district disruption. JHU’s Modern Classrooms study likewise found stronger observed HQIM fidelity among MCP teachers, but was nonrandom and had not yet measured student outcomes. CommonLit provides a promising descriptive usage-outcome signal, while ANet’s student-impact analysis remains prospective.

What's new or sharper in 2026

Improved curriculum use is a meaningful proximal result, but it cannot yet serve as a proxy for student impact.

Implication for the portfolio

Preserve separate measures and decision thresholds for participation, teacher practice, instructional quality, dosage, and student outcomes; fund longer-term tests that connect these stages.

Differentiated Supports
Which tutoring and supplemental models achieve the strongest impact per dollar — and under what conditions does lighter-touch or technology-mediated delivery preserve outcomes as costs fall?
Differentiated Supports · School Success
Live-online human tutoring is the portfolio’s strongest current efficiency signal, but not yet a proven universally superior model

PLI reports that virtual tutoring produced impacts comparable to, and in some analyses larger than, in-person tutoring at roughly half the cost. Saga provides a concrete example: its live-online blended model cost approximately $2,510 per student, compared with $2,680 for in-person blended and $4,930 for traditional in-person 2:1 tutoring; the virtual model increased Algebra 1 Regents pass rates by about eight percentage points while delivering an average of 69 tutoring hours. However, PLI cautions that modality, subject, grade, dosage, staffing, and other design features were bundled rather than independently randomized, and Saga’s model remains expensive in absolute terms.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The evidence now points to live-online delivery, rather than reduced human support, as the most plausible near-term efficiency lever, but does not establish that virtual delivery itself caused the stronger cost-effectiveness.

Implication for the portfolio

Prioritize comparisons of live-online and in-person human tutoring using common outcome, dosage, quality, and all-in cost measures. Do not treat “virtual” as synonymous with low-touch or low-cost.

Sources · Saga Education Grantee Notes
Differentiated Supports · School Success
Apparent cost reductions often come from changing dosage, ratios, or who bears the implementation work, not from making the same model more efficient

Saga estimates that ratio, student count, and dosage drive roughly two-thirds of delivery cost, while human capital accounts for about 80 percent of total cost. Its New Mexico model targets just over $1,000 per student by moving to 4:1 groups, 40-minute sessions three times weekly, larger coaching caseloads, and more district responsibility, but that estimate excludes administrator costs and outcomes are not yet available. Chapter One met a $550 cost-per-student target, yet its broader grade 2–3 outcome improved only about five percent; substantially stronger results came from a selected fluency-pilot subgroup without a comparison group.

What's new or sharper in 2026

“Lower cost” is now clearly a change in model architecture and cost allocation, not merely an efficiency gain within a stable intervention.

Implication for the portfolio

Require an all-in cost ledger that includes provider and district staffing, supervision, facilities, technology, scheduling, and coordination. Pair it with model-specific outcomes and explicitly document which components changed.

Differentiated Supports · School Success
The decision-useful scale metric is the share of target students who reach meaningful dosage, not enrollment or average hours among participants

PLI finds a linear association between tutoring hours and learning gains, although it does not establish a causal or universal threshold. In Saga’s NYC implementation, students were offered an average of 102 hours but received 71, with wide variation across schools; reporting only the participant average obscures students who never entered or persisted in the model. Accelerate therefore recommends tracking the percentage of eligible or targeted students who actually receive intended dosage, while Chapter One’s selected fluency subgroup illustrates how strong outcomes among students who reached a component can coexist with modest program-wide results.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The portfolio can now define scale more rigorously as target-population reach at dosage, rather than treating reach and dosage as separate success measures.

Students who receive more dosage may also attend more regularly, experience stronger implementation, or attend schools with greater capacity, making it difficult to separate dosage from the conditions producing it.

Most importantly, the reviewed evidence does not establish a dosage level that produces positive effects across all achievement groups. PLI’s subgroup and model findings are useful for generating hypotheses, but bundled and endogenously selected program features limit interpretation. Existing targets are therefore best understood as implementation benchmarks to test, not validated causal thresholds.

What's new or sharper in 2026

OFF gained more explicit dosage definitions and several dosage-outcome signals, but the accumulated evidence made the lack of comparable, validated thresholds more visible. The learning advanced from “dosage matters” to “dosage must be defined and validated within each model and population.”

Implication for the portfolio

OFF should ask grantees to justify dosage targets, report the full distribution of use, and analyze outcomes across dosage levels and student subgroups. Studies should address selection into higher dosage and test whether different students require different amounts or types of exposure. Portfolio-wide thresholds should not be established from provider-defined targets.

Implementation Fidelity · Research
The strongest implementation evidence treats ownership, protected time, technical readiness, supervision, and progress monitoring as components of the intervention rather than optional supports around it.

The clearest causal example comes from an RCT of Khan Academy across 83 residential government middle schools in Uttar Pradesh. A dedicated lab-in-charge increased use from 7.2 to 47.4 minutes per week and raised mathematics achievement by nearly 0.5 SD over 31 weeks. The implementation lead secured connectivity, simplified rostering, protected time, supervised students, coordinated with teachers, and monitored progress. The study validates this bundled model in its setting, although it does not identify which individual component produced the effect.

Other evidence supports the bundle’s plausibility. In Digital Promise’s outcomes-based contracting work, districts using mutual accountability, clear cross-functional roles, school-team involvement, real-time monitoring, and iterative implementation planning achieved provider-defined dosage for 50–95% of students and approximately ten times the usage typical of conventional contracts. Zearn’s Louisiana assurance districts, which combined superintendent engagement, coaching, data transparency, launch readiness, and protected routines, reported 31% growth in weekly lesson completion compared with a 3% decline in non-assurance districts. These latter comparisons are descriptive and do not establish student impact.

The common mechanism is operational: someone has authority and responsibility to translate a tool into schedules, staffing, technical access, student supervision, data review, and corrective action. Nominally low-cost technology may therefore require substantial implementation infrastructure.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The Khan Academy study provided unusually strong evidence that implementation ownership can cause both greater use and improved outcomes. The broader portfolio supplied corroborating evidence that accountability and monitoring are most effective when paired with school-level capacity to act.

Implication for the portfolio

OFF should treat implementation ownership and protected routines as part of intervention design and cost. Grants and evaluations should specify who performs each implementation function, what data they receive, and how they respond to low usage. Future research should test the transferability and cost of the bundled model and, where feasible, isolate its active components.

Implementation Fidelity · Research
Initial training rarely secures durable implementation; sustained fidelity appears to require coaching, peer support, responsive problem-solving, and leadership capacity at multiple organizational levels.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The evidence became more specific about what follows initial training. The strongest recurring conditions are early follow-up, job-embedded coaching, peer support, administrator awareness, and distributed capacity that can survive staffing or operational disruptions.

OFF should ask grantees to define the support arc after launch, including how implementation problems are identified and resolved and when intensive support can responsibly step down. Reaching dosage and changing proximal practice are necessary but insufficient for student impact; fidelity must include the quality and coherence of what happens during use.

The AIR/Teaching Lab RCT provides the clearest caution. Among participating treatment teachers, 76% completed all 20 professional-learning hours and averaged 21.6 coaching sessions. Modern Classrooms demonstrated stronger observed fidelity, while student outcomes remain pending.

These findings indicate that implementation fidelity is multidimensional. It includes exposure, but also instructional quality, curriculum alignment, appropriate student support, responsiveness to data, and sufficient time for changed practice to influence learning.

The portfolio gained a strong null or mixed example showing that intensive participation can alter teacher practice without producing a detectable student effect over one year. How should Future Readiness be measured to capture durable cognition and later behavior rather than immediate attitudes — and what validation pathway is needed to make new measures decision-relevant?
Future Readiness · Research
Current measures often capture immediate attitudes, self-perceptions, or participation rather than durable cognition or later behavior, making them weak evidence of future readiness on their own.

Much of the current Future Readiness evidence relies on self-report surveys, post-program measures, participation counts, or short-term indicators such as confidence and interest. These measures can identify an immediate response to an experience, but they do not establish that students developed durable cognitive skills or will behave differently later.

The Challenger Center RCT illustrates this limitation. The experience produced immediate gains in STEM self-efficacy and career attitudes, but those effects were no longer statistically significant six months later. The study found no significant effects on subsequent afterschool or summer STEM participation or seventh-grade STEM course-taking. Learn Fresh provides a different boundary: its RCT found positive mathematics effects of approximately 0.15–0.20 SD but no significant effects on exploratory measures of math self-efficacy, value of math, self-management, or self-awareness. Academic impact and measured Future Readiness impact therefore diverged within the same intervention.

These findings do not mean attitudes or self-reports lack value. They can capture important intermediate constructs. But they should not be treated as evidence of durable cognition unless studies demonstrate that they predict later problem-solving, persistence, learning behavior, course-taking, or performance.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The portfolio gained longitudinal and causal counterexamples showing that immediate attitude changes can fade and that academic gains do not automatically produce measurable Future Readiness gains. This sharpened the distinction between proximal perceptions and durable cognitive or behavioral outcomes.

Implication for the portfolio

OFF should require Future Readiness studies to identify whether each measure represents perception, demonstrated skill, or later behavior. Self-report should be paired with performance tasks, observable behavior, student work, and longitudinal follow-up whenever the theory of change depends on persistence or transfer.

Future Readiness · Research
Future Readiness findings are highly sensitive to task format, context, and developmental stage; a single measure can substantially understate or overstate what students learned.

HGSE’s interactive-media research demonstrates how outcome format affects conclusions. Among approximately 150 completers, the interactive condition showed a preliminary overall advantage that was stronger on multiple-choice outcomes and among children ages six to eight. No comparable advantage appeared on open-ended items, despite approximately 95% of participants meeting the minimum dosage requirement. Preliminary analyses also distinguish responding to an AI prompt from generating an original question, with child-generated inquiry potentially providing a stronger signal of deeper learning.

This means that high participation or improved closed-form scores may coexist with limited evidence of transfer, explanation, or independent reasoning. A measure may be reliable while still capturing only recognition, prompted response, or task familiarity. Age and language demands can further influence whether an assessment reflects the intended cognitive skill or students’ ability to understand and navigate the assessment format.

Current portfolio tools also vary in whether they measure a general skill, performance in a particular task, or behavior within one program. Without evidence that scores generalize across settings, it is difficult to know whether observed gains represent durable cognition or context-specific performance.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The evidence moved beyond the general concern that measures are imperfect. It showed concretely that the same intervention can appear effective on one outcome format and ineffective on another, even with high dosage. This makes triangulation a validity requirement rather than an optional methodological enhancement.

Implication for the portfolio

OFF should avoid relying on a single outcome format. Future studies should combine selected-response measures with open-ended tasks, student-generated questions, observable problem-solving behavior, and artifacts of student work. They should also test measurement invariance across age, language, demographic groups, and program contexts.

Future Readiness · Research
New Future Readiness measures need a staged validation pathway linking reliable scores to authentic behavior and meaningful later outcomes; this requires shared longitudinal data infrastructure, not isolated tool development.

Reliability and scalable scoring are necessary but insufficient. A Future Readiness measure must first define the intended construct, score it consistently, operate fairly across student groups, detect meaningful change, and remain valid across settings. It must then demonstrate a relationship to consequential later outcomes such as subsequent learning behavior, course-taking, persistence, educational performance, or workforce-relevant activity.

ETS’s planned validation work reflects much of this sequence, including reliability of at least 0.80, interrater agreement, differential-item-functioning analysis, construct representation, and relationships with GPA, attendance, and academic benchmarks. Opportunity Insights provides a useful standard from academic measurement: measures become more decision-relevant when they demonstrate predictive relationships with later performance. Its current evidence concerns academic measures and selective-college outcomes, however, not OFF’s Future Readiness constructs.

Establishing similar predictive validity for persistence, problem-solving, inquiry, or collaboration is difficult because the necessary individual-level longitudinal data are fragmented or inaccessible. Privacy constraints, limited data-sharing agreements, and short study periods make it unrealistic for each grantee to validate a new measure independently against distal outcomes.

What's new or sharper in 2026

The measurement problem became clearer as both a psychometric and infrastructure challenge. The field needs better instruments, but it also needs longitudinal datasets and common validation standards that allow promising measures to be compared and linked to later behavior.

Does coherence across in-school and out-of-school experiences improve student connection and outcomes — and which features of HQIM alignment produce the strongest implementation and impact?
Instructional Coherence · Research
No completed OFF-reviewed study yet demonstrates that greater coherence across in- and out-of-school experiences improves connection, attendance, or other student outcomes.

Detailed lesson

The current evidence base includes studies of attendance interventions, out-of-school participation, and instructional alignment, but no completed study directly tests whether connecting students’ in-school and out-of-school experiences improves attendance, belonging, relationships, or another measure of connection.

UC Irvine’s Learner Pathways Analysis is beginning to build the measurement infrastructure needed to answer this question. It distinguishes opportunity coverage, depth and breadth of participation, re-enrollment, and transitions into and out of STEM experiences, with plans to link those pathways to course-taking, grades, and assessments. However, primary data collection has been delayed, and the study has not established which pathway patterns predict persistence or connection.

The Challenger Center RCT found no significant effect on subsequent afterschool or summer STEM participation, but it tested a single experiential program rather than coherence between settings. Attendance studies likewise test messaging, health supports, or relational interventions rather than whether alignment with out-of-school experiences affects attendance.

The evidence therefore supports a measurement and research agenda, not a substantive conclusion that cross-setting coherence improves connection.

What's new or sharper in 2026

OFF gained a clearer framework for measuring pathways across settings. The evidence now suggests that one-time enrollment or attendance is insufficient; coherence may need to be represented through repeated participation, transitions, continuity, and connections between experiences. Whether those patterns improve connection remains untested.

Implication for the portfolio

OFF should fund a direct test of cross-setting coherence. A credible study would define coherence operationally, compare students receiving coordinated and uncoordinated experiences, and track belonging, relationships, attendance, repeated participation, course-taking, and academic outcomes over time. Until then, cross-setting coherence should remain a hypothesis rather than a portfolio lesson about impact.
